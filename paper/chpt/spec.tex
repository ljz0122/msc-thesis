\section{Spectroscopic data sources}

Since January 2025, we have observed 88 targets from our selected list of quasar candidates. These observations are taken by two telescopes, Nordic Optical Telescope (NOT) and Gran Telescopio CANARIAS (GTC). Both of them are located in La Palma, Spain.

\subsection{NOT}

The Nordic Optical Telescope (NOT), located at La Palma in Canary Islands, is a joint program by astronomers from the Nordic countries. All of SNAQS observations on NOT were taken with The Alhambra Faint Object Spectrograph and Camera (ALFOSC). All targets were observed with the grism~\#4 and \qty{1}{\arcsecond} slit, which covers the wavelengths \qtyrange{3200}{9600}{\angstrom} with a spectral resolution $R=360$. Spectral resolution ($R$) is a criteria to demonstrate the instrument's ability to distinguish smaller details in electromagnetic spectra and it's defined by: \[R = \frac{\lambda}{\Delta\lambda}\] where $\Delta\lambda$ is the smallest difference in wavelength that can be distinguished at the wavelength $\lambda$.

\subsection{GTC}

The Gran Telescopio Canarias is also located at La Palma in Canary Islands as NOT. All observations from GTC were taken with the upgraded Optical System for Imaging and low-Intermediate-Resolution Integrated Spectroscopy (OSIRIS+). Furthermore all the observations were performed with the grism \texttt{R1000B}, which covers wavelengths \qtyrange{3630}{7000}{\angstrom} with a spectral resolution $R=1018$.

\subsection{Preprocessing}

\subsubsection{BIAS Frames}

Bias frames are zero-second exposures taken with the telescope shutter closed. They record the fixed zero-point electronic offset of the CCD along with the associated readout noise. To reduce this part of signal baseline from our science images, a series of raw bias frames are acquired -- usually at the beginning or end of an observation night -- and combined (such as median combination) into a high signal-to-noise Master Bias frame (see Figure \ref{fig:master_bias}).
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{paper//figs//spec//reduction/bias.png}
    \caption{Master Bias Frame for Observation Date 2026-02-17}
    \label{fig:master_bias}
\end{figure}

While this master frame maps the spatial structure of the bias level across the detector, the overall bias level can still fluctuate throughout the whole night. To correct for these temporal variations between the science frames and the bias frames were taken, an overscan correction is typically applied using the overscan region of each individual frame.\citep{alma99123053678205763}

Additionally, there is no bias frames for some observation days. But it's stable based on the Quality Monitoring\footnote{https://www.not.iac.es/instruments/qc/alfosc.php} on NOT Website. Consequently, we used the closest day's frame to do the reduction. 

\subsubsection{Arc Frames}

To connect the pixel on CCD to a certain wavelength, arc frames were acquired before and after the science exposures using the identical grism configuration. These calibration frames were generated by illuminating the light path with an arc lamp which could produce a series of known wavelength light. The resulting spectra are used to establish the dispersion solution, mapping the detector pixels to physical wavelengths. For observations at the Nordic Optical Telescope (NOT), a HeNe (Helium-Neon) lamp was utilized. 

To get a higher signal-to-noise arc frame, we could combine multiple arc frames (like Figure~\ref{fig:master_arc}).

\subsubsection{FLAT Frames}

Understanding flat frames requires defining three key terms: Analog-to-Digital Units (ADU), gain (the number of ADU could be produced by 1 electron), and Quantum Efficiency (QE, the ratio of detected to incident photons) \citep{alma99123053678205763}. Individual CCD pixels exhibit inherent variations in gain and QE. Consequently, even under uniform illumination, the output signal varies across the detector \citep{alma99123053678205763}. Flat frames are acquired to correct this pixel-to-pixel sensitivity variation. 
Similar to bias and arc frames, multiple flat frames are combined to generate a master flat frame. This master flat frame is then normalized by dividing each pixel by the frame's global average value. In the resulting normalized flat field, a value of 1 represents the detector's average sensitivity, and variations reflect relative differences in pixel QE\citep{alma99123033908205763}. \subsubsection{Reduction}

Once the master calibration frames (bias, arc, and flat) have been generated, the pipeline could proceed to the primary reduction of the scientific observation frames. This process applies the previously discussed corrections: for each raw science frame, the master bias is subtracted to remove the base signal, and the result is divided by the normalized master flat field to correct for pixel-to-pixel sensitivity variations. This standard calibration is applied to all observation frames, yielding reduced data ready for subsequent pipeline steps, such as wavelength calibration and spectral extraction. \subsection{1D Spectra}

Following the above 2D reduction steps, the final phase of the data processing pipeline involves extracting and calibrating the one-dimensional (1D) spectra. This essential procedure transforms the fully corrected 2D spectroscopic images into scientifically actionable data, representing flux as a function of physical wavelength. 

\subsubsection{1D Spectrum Extraction}

The primary objective of this step is to collapse the spatial profile of the target on the 2D detector into a single 1D spectrum while maximizing the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and minimizing contamination from the outside pixels. 

First, the spatial position of the target trace along the dispersion axis is mapped. Once traced, the spectrum is typically extracted using an optimal extraction algorithm  \citep[following][]{1986PASP...98..609H}. Unlike just a simple aperture extraction (which uniformly sums all pixels within a spatial window), optimal extraction applies a spatial weighting profile based on the empirical Point Spread Function (PSF) of the target. This method heavily weights the central pixels where the target signal is strongest and down-weights the noisy outer wings. Concurrently, the local sky background is estimated from adjacent spatial regions devoid of target emission and subtracted, while residual cosmic rays are identified and masked out through variance analysis. Figure~\ref{fig:objtrace} is a example of object tracing and Figure~\ref{fig:1dspec} is an example of an extracted 1D spectrum.

To improve the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of spectrum, we usually take multiple frames for a single target. Then we can combine the frames with Inverse Variance Weighted Regression (IVWR), which is defined by: \[{\hat {y}}={\frac {\sum _{i}y_{i}/\sigma _{i}^{2}}{\sum _{i}1/\sigma _{i}^{2}}}\]
and variance is \[Var({\hat {y}})={\frac {1}{\sum _{i}1/\sigma _{i}^{2}}}\]

\section{Spectral Analysis}

The quasar spectrum template from \citet{selsing_x-shooter_2016} is used to classify the quasars and identify the redshift of them. The Python package \texttt{dust\_extinction} developed by \citet{gordonDust_extinctionInterstellarDust2024} is applied to calculate the dust extinction effect. The extinction curve we used in this analyze is from \citet{ysardTHEMIS20Selfconsistent2024}.

\subsection{Automatic Match}

We used \texttt{emcee} \citep{foreman-mackeyEmceeMCMCHammer2013} to run MCMC (Markov Chain Monte Carlo) to find the best fitted redshift and extinction parameters. This method is based on random walks and aim to find out the model with maximum likelihood. This programme is pushed on my GitHub repository\footnote{https://github.com/ljz0122/msc-thesis\label{fn:gh-repo}}.

To reduce the contamination from the telluric absorption lines, we masked the spectra at wavelength \qtyrange{6860}{6915}{\angstrom} ($\mathrm{O}_2$ B-band) and \qtyrange{7590}{7690}{\angstrom} ($\mathrm{O}_2$ A-band) in automatic fitting. It's much harder to remove telluric absorption lines than the emission ones, because it requires a more complex model to fit the absorption lines in spectra. While the software \texttt{molecfit} developed by \citet{smetteMolecfitGeneralTool2015} is a tool to do this and has been tested on ESO's instruments, it requires too much time to make it work on the spectra from NOT/ALFOSC (e.g. get compatible weather data). So we just simply masked the telluric absorption area to avoid its influence on automatic processing.
